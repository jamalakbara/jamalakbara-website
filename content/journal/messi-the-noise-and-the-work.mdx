There's a kind of noise that follows anyone trying to build something. Not the constructive kind — the other kind. The takes. The comparisons. The people who decide what your work means before they've actually looked at it. The accusations that attach themselves to any result, good or bad, and find the failure regardless.

I've been building things for a while now, and I've never found a clean way to deal with that noise. What I've found instead is a person who showed me what it looks like to not let it in. His name is Lionel Messi, and after what happened at MetLife Stadium last Sunday, I feel like I finally understand why he's mattered to me the way he has.

Spain beat Argentina 1-0 in the World Cup final. Ferran Torres in the 106th minute. Messi — 39 years old, barely in the match — collected his runners-up medal and walked toward the Argentina fans still in the stands instead of going to the locker room. That's where the tears came. And the crowd chanted his name.

He lost. The crowd chanted anyway.

## What mastery actually looks like

I don't remember the exact match where I first really watched Messi. I remember the feeling — someone moving through a game like they were reading it two seconds ahead of everyone else. Not faster. Not necessarily stronger. Just like the space he needed always appeared exactly where he was going.

That image has stayed with me in ways I didn't expect. When I'm working on something and it starts to click — when the thing I'm building stops feeling forced and starts feeling like it's finding its own shape — that's the only analogy I have for it. Not struggle. Inevitability.

The dangerous part is that effortlessness looks like not trying. When Messi played well for Barcelona and then Argentina lost, the read was that he was holding back. That he didn't care. I see the same thing happen in creative work: when something looks polished, people assume it was easy, assume the thought wasn't there. The craft disappears into the result. And when the result isn't what people hoped, the assumption is you weren't really in it.

You don't score 919 career goals by not trying. You don't ship work you're proud of by coasting. The effort is there — it just stopped being visible, which is what actually getting good at something looks like.

## The noise problem

Here's what I've noticed about building things publicly: the criticism doesn't stop when you succeed. It adapts.

I watched this happen to Messi for a decade. Score 91 goals in a calendar year — fine, but what about Argentina? Lead Argentina to a World Cup final — fine, but he didn't perform well enough *in* the final. Win eight Ballon d'Ors — the votes were rigged, FIFA had a preferred narrative, the football establishment was protecting its asset. There was always something. The machine found the failure regardless of what happened.

And it wasn't just the Ballon d'Or. Everything he touched eventually got a cheating angle attached to it. Referees giving Argentina soft decisions. VAR going their way. FIFA protecting the golden goose. When he announced he was retiring from Argentina's national team in 2016 — after another Copa América final loss — and then came back two months later, even that became ammunition. He couldn't commit. He was inconsistent. The criticism followed every decision he made, including the ones that directly contradicted the last criticism.

I think about this in my own work constantly. The people who say what you're doing doesn't count, or that you got lucky, or that the context made it easier — they don't stop when you prove them wrong. They find the next angle. Understanding that early would have saved me a lot of energy I spent trying to respond to takes that were never actually about the work.

What I got from watching Messi through those years: you say your piece once, if at all, and then go back to work. That's the whole strategy.

## The moment I actually cried

I watched the 2022 World Cup final alone. I don't cry at football often. I cried at that one.

Argentina vs France. Messi scores twice. 2-0. Then Mbappé — two goals in 97 seconds in the 80th and 82nd minutes. Into extra time. Messi scores again in the 108th. Mbappé completes a hat trick in the 118th. 3-3. Argentina win the penalty shootout 4-2.

Messi wins the Golden Ball for the second time. The only player in World Cup history to do that.

I cried because I'd spent years watching this person be told he couldn't do it, and here he was doing it at 35, in the most dramatic final ever played. There's a specific feeling you get when someone you've believed in gets to prove it — not to you, to everyone. That was the night.

And then the accusations started immediately. A video went viral of FIFA president Gianni Infantino telling Argentine players he'd "suffered with them" during the tournament — proof, people said, that FIFA had a side. FIFA had appointed an all-Argentine officiating crew for the France vs Morocco quarterfinal. Pepe publicly claimed Messi had influence over referees after Portugal was knocked out. Egypt complained about "double standards" from officials. The "rigged for Pessi" take spread everywhere.

He'd won the thing people said he couldn't win, in the most impossible final imaginable. Half the world immediately decided it didn't count.

What I took from that: the goal posts move. When you've done the thing people said you couldn't do, the story becomes that you did it the wrong way. It never ends. The only stable thing is whether *you* know what the work was.

He could have retired that night. He didn't.

## One sentence

The 2026 World Cup was everything the previous one was, louder. Defending champions. Every accusation amplified by the previous one.

After Argentina beat Egypt, Egypt filed a formal FIFA complaint — not a social media post, an actual legal document — claiming "serious refereeing mistakes," "double standards," and deliberate VAR manipulation. Argentina had the highest rate of VAR overturns in their favor of any team in the tournament. Cristiano Ronaldo liked a post calling them referee darlings. FIFA's chief of refereeing had to hold a press conference to defend the officiating. Scaloni, Argentina's coach, spent press conferences addressing "the rigged narrative" instead of talking about football.

Messi responded once, to all of it: *"We've been the best over these past four years, either you like it or not. Nothing was handed to us."*

One sentence. Then back to work. Argentina made the final — third time with him in it, 2014, 2022, 2026.

I've written that sentence down. Kept it. There's a discipline in that kind of economy I'm still learning — the ability to say your piece once, clearly, and not need to repeat it or elaborate or keep fighting. When you're in the middle of something you know is real, you don't need to convince everyone. You need to finish the work.

## The crowd at MetLife Stadium

Spain were better on the day. Messi had 15 touches all match. Enzo Fernández got a second yellow. Ferran Torres scored in extra time. 1-0. That's the game.

Messi leaves the pitch in tears. Collects the runners-up medal. And instead of going to the tunnel, walks toward the Argentina fans in the stands. Stands there. Cries. The crowd chants his name.

I've been thinking about what that moment means all week, and I keep coming back to this: the crowd wasn't chanting for the result. They were chanting for a little boy from Rosario who became the measuring stick for an entire generation of football — and who got there not by making the critics stop, but by making the work impossible to ignore.

That's the thing I want to carry. Not "ignore the haters" — that's too simple and honestly too passive. It's more specific than that. It's: the work you keep doing, consistently, in the same direction, becomes something that a single result can't erase. Or a decade of takes. Or a formal FIFA complaint. Or Ronaldo liking a post.

The noise was relentless for twenty years. It never got in.

That's what I want to build toward. Not a career without critics — I don't think that's possible for anyone doing anything worth doing. But work that accumulates weight the critics can't match. Work that earns a chant even when the result goes wrong.

A little boy from Rosario. Twenty years. The crowd still chanting.
